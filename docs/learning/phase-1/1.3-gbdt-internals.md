# 1.3 How gradient-boosted trees actually split

**Phase:** 1 · **Date:** 2026-09-14 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** The small split calculation below is illustrative arithmetic, not a result.
> Model numbers come from the synthetic run of `bastion train`; IEEE-CIS results are **unmeasured**.

## What problem does this solve?

Payment data is tabular and messy: amounts spanning four orders of magnitude, counts, ratios, flags,
categories, and many missing values. The useful signal lives in interactions ("new device *and* high
amount"). We need a model that finds those interactions without hand-crafted crosses, handles
missing values, ranks well, scores in milliseconds on a CPU, and can explain each score. Gradient-
boosted decision trees do all of that.

## How it works

**Boosting fits the mistakes.** The model is a sum of small trees. Each new tree is fit to the
*gradient* of the loss for the current predictions. For binary log loss, with raw prediction *f*,
probability *p = sigmoid(f)* and label *y*:

- gradient `g = p - y` (how wrong, and in which direction)
- hessian `h = p(1 - p)` (how confident, which scales the step)

**A leaf's value** minimises the second-order approximation of the loss: `w = -G / (H + λ)`, where G and
H sum the gradients and hessians of the rows in the leaf, and λ is L2 regularisation (`lambda_l2: 1.0`).

**A split's gain** compares the loss improvement of two children with one parent:

```
gain = ½ [ G_L²/(H_L+λ) + G_R²/(H_R+λ) − (G_L+G_R)²/(H_L+H_R+λ) ]
```

*Illustrative example.* Start from the base rate, p = 0.01 everywhere. Take 10,000 transactions, 100
of them fraud, and consider splitting on `card_txn_count_1m > 2`:

- Right child: 150 rows, 60 of them fraud. G_R = 150·0.01 − 60 = −58.5; H_R = 150·0.0099 = 1.485.
- Left child: 9,850 rows, 40 fraud. G_L = 98.5 − 40 = 58.5; H_L = 97.515.
- Parent: G = 0; H = 99.0.
- gain = ½ [58.5²/98.515 + 58.5²/2.485 − 0] ≈ ½ [34.7 + 1377.2] ≈ **706**.

The small right child dominates the gain: it concentrates a lot of gradient in few rows. That is
exactly how a model finds rare patterns like card testing.

**How LightGBM makes this fast** (Ke et al., 2017):

- **Histograms.** Each feature is bucketed into at most 255 bins (`max_bin`), so finding a split scans
  bins, not rows.
- **Leaf-wise growth.** It always splits the leaf with the largest gain, up to `num_leaves: 63`. That
  gives deeper, more accurate trees than level-wise growth for the same number of leaves, and it
  overfits faster on small data, hence `min_data_in_leaf: 100`.
- **Missing values** learn a default direction at each split. That is why Bastion's null features
  (a device age with no device, a z-score with no history) need no imputation.
- **Categoricals** (merchant category, email domains) are split natively, by grouping levels
  according to their gradient statistics, rather than one-hot encoded.

**In Bastion** (`bastion.training.models.train_lightgbm`):

- Learning rate 0.05, early stopping on validation PR-AUC with 100 rounds of patience. The synthetic
  run stopped at iteration 384 of a maximum 3,000.
- No class weighting. Reweighting fraud would push probabilities up. Cost asymmetry belongs in the
  expected-loss policy, and probability accuracy belongs to calibration (ADR-005, ADR-006).
- Deterministic mode with a fixed thread count, so a rerun reproduces the model.

**What the synthetic model leaned on** (share of total gain): `card_txn_count_1m` 36.0%, `amount`
26.7%, `amount_to_card_mean_30d` 13.6%, `card_merchant_seen_before` 9.1%. Bursts of probes, odd
amounts, and unfamiliar merchants: the patterns the generator was built with.

## Why we chose it here

- **ADR-002:** on tabular, heterogeneous, imbalanced data, GBDTs match or beat deep models at a
  fraction of the effort, train in seconds to minutes, and score in milliseconds on a CPU.
  Grinsztajn et al. (2022) document why trees still win on tabular data.
- **TreeSHAP** gives exact per-row feature attributions for tree ensembles, which the analyst console
  needs (Phase 4).

## What we rejected and why

- **Logistic regression as the primary model.** It is kept as a baseline. On synthetic data it
  reached PR-AUC 0.9485 [0.927, 0.964] against LightGBM's 1.0000; with both near the ceiling the gap
  is not decisive. The IEEE-CIS comparison is unmeasured.
- **Tabular deep learning.** Slower and more fragile, with no expected accuracy gain here, plus a
  harder 50 ms CPU serving budget.
- **XGBoost.** A close sibling, and a fine choice. LightGBM's histogram and leaf-wise defaults and
  native categorical support fit wide IEEE-CIS tables better out of the box.

## Where it breaks

- **Trees don't extrapolate.** An amount far beyond anything seen in training lands in the edge
  bin and scores like the largest amount the model knows.
- **Gain importance flatters high-cardinality and continuous features**, and it spreads credit
  arbitrarily among correlated features. Use it to spot shortcuts, not to rank causes.
- **Leaf-wise growth overfits small windows.** With few frauds, `min_data_in_leaf` and early stopping
  do real work.
- **Order-dependent behaviour** is invisible unless features encode it. A card's *sequence* of
  merchants is exactly where ADR-002 says a sequence model would be revisited.

## The interview answer

"Boosting fits each new tree to the gradient of the log loss: basically the residual p − y, scaled by
the model's confidence. A split's gain rewards pulling concentrated gradient into a small leaf, which
is how a few card-testing bursts get their own branch. LightGBM makes that fast with histograms and
leaf-wise growth, handles missing values and categoricals natively, and scores on a CPU in
milliseconds. I don't reweight classes: cost belongs in the decision policy, and probability quality
belongs to calibration."
