# 1.5 Calibration, and why the policy engine needs it

**Phase:** 1 · **Date:** 2026-09-14 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** Measured values come from two synthetic runs of `bastion train`: before and
> after the generator fix described in `docs/learning/mistakes.md`. The review-decision example is
> illustrative arithmetic using the assumed costs in `configs/costs.yaml`. IEEE-CIS calibration is
> **unmeasured**.

## What problem does this solve?

The policy engine (Phase 4) compares expected costs, and expected fraud loss is **probability ×
amount**. That multiplication only makes sense if a score of 0.02 really means "2 of every 100 such
transactions are fraud". A model can rank fraud perfectly and still be badly miscalibrated. Ranking
metrics would never notice; the loss calculation would be wrong on every transaction.

## How it works

*Illustrative decision.* A 2,000 USD transaction scored 0.02:

- expected fraud loss if approved: 0.02 × 2,000 = **40 USD**
- analyst review: **5 USD** (assumed), which catches most of that loss
- so reviewing is the cheaper action.

If the true risk were ten times lower (0.2%), the expected loss would be 4 USD and reviewing would
waste money. The model's *ranking* is identical in both cases. Only calibration tells the policy which
world it is in.

**Measuring it** (`bastion.evaluation.calibration`):

- **Brier score:** mean of (p − y)². It mixes calibration and ranking. Lower is better.
- **Log loss:** heavily punishes confident mistakes, and a probability of exactly 0 for something that
  happens is punished without limit.
- **Expected calibration error (ECE):** sort transactions by score into 15 equal-size bins, then take
  the count-weighted average gap between each bin's mean predicted probability and its observed fraud
  rate.
- **Reliability figure:** predicted vs observed per bin. With fraud near 1%, equal-mass bins put almost
  every bin in the low-risk bulk, so the figure uses log-spaced bins of at least 50 rows instead.

**Fixing it**, always on data the model was not trained on (the `calibration` window):

- **Platt scaling** fits a logistic regression to the logit of the raw score. Two parameters, smooth,
  monotone, and it assumes the distortion is sigmoid-shaped.
- **Isotonic regression** fits any non-decreasing step function (pool-adjacent-violators). It is
  flexible, but it can overfit when positives are few.

**What we measured.** On the first synthetic run, isotonic calibration made things *worse* than the raw
scores. PR-AUC fell from 0.9998 to 0.9863 and log loss rose from 0.0005 to 0.0040; Platt matched the
raw scores. The cause is structural. Isotonic regression assigns a probability of exactly **0** to any
score range with no fraud in the calibration window. A fraud that later lands in that range costs
unbounded log loss, and to a policy engine it looks like a transaction safe to approve.

So Bastion **selects** the calibrator (`calibration: select`). Both methods are fit on the earlier 70%
of the calibration window and scored by log loss on the most recent 30%; the winner is refit on the
whole window. The test window takes no part. On the revised synthetic generator the selection chose
**Platt** (holdout log loss 0.00016 vs 0.00022 for isotonic). On the test window: log loss 0.0002 raw,
0.0003 Platt, 0.0004 isotonic.

## Why we chose it here

- **ADR-006:** probabilities are calibrated before the policy engine may use them, with Brier score
  and a reliability figure reported.
- Selection by recent-window log loss is a small, justified deviation from the roadmap's "isotonic",
  recorded in ARCHITECTURE.md §7 together with the measurement that prompted it.
- Calibrators are stored as JSON (isotonic thresholds; Platt slope and intercept). Serving applies
  them with `np.interp` or one sigmoid: no pickles, no scikit-learn.

## What we rejected and why

- **Raw scores as probabilities.** Nothing guarantees them. Early stopping, subsampling and
  regularisation all change the scale, and so does drift.
- **Calibrating on training data.** The model is overconfident on rows it has already seen.
- **Choosing the calibrator on the test window.** That is test-set selection, however harmless it
  looks.
- **Class reweighting to "help" rare fraud.** It deliberately shifts probabilities away from the true
  rate, which is the opposite of what the policy needs.

## Where it breaks

- **Calibration drifts.** A calibrator fit last month is wrong once the fraud rate or the fraud mix
  changes. Phase 6 monitors calibration on matured labels.
- **Few positives make isotonic brittle.** That is exactly the zero-probability failure above.
- **Calibration is a marginal property.** A model can be calibrated overall and still miscalibrated
  for, say, transactions above 1,000 USD, which are the ones the policy cares about most. Per-segment
  reliability checks would catch it.
- **Near-perfect separation makes the reliability figure degenerate.** On synthetic data the
  well-populated bins sit at 0 or at 1, so the figure has little to say. IEEE-CIS should produce a
  real curve.

## The interview answer

"My decision policy multiplies probability by amount, so a score of 0.7 has to mean 70%. I calibrate on
a held-out window and measure Brier score, log loss and reliability. Isotonic calibration hurt on my
first run: with few frauds it assigned zero probability to score ranges where fraud later happened,
which a cost-based policy would read as safe to approve. So I pick between isotonic and Platt by log
loss on the most recent slice of the calibration window, never on test, and store the calibrator as
plain JSON for serving."
