# 0.2 Why a rules baseline comes first

**Phase:** 0 · **Date:** 2026-09-14 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** The worked example is Bastion's synthetic dataset (seed 7, 183 days,
> 3,000 cards), measured with `bastion baseline rules`. IEEE-CIS rules-baseline results are
> **unmeasured** until the dataset is downloaded.

## What problem does this solve?

A model's metric means nothing without something to compare it with. "PR-AUC 0.6" might be superb
or worse than five if-statements. Fraud teams ran rules long before machine learning, and many
still do, so the honest question for any model is: **does it lose less money than the rules it
would replace?** The rules baseline turns that question into a number that every later model must
beat (ROADMAP Phase 0: "a model that can't beat this is worthless").

## How it works

**Five rules** (`bastion.rules.baseline`), using only canonical fields and shared features:

| Rule | Fires when |
|---|---|
| `high_amount` | amount above a high quantile of legitimate amounts |
| `velocity_1h` | more card transactions in the previous hour than legitimate traffic usually shows |
| `new_device_high_amount` | device never used by this card before, and a high amount |
| `amount_spike` | ≥ 3 card transactions in the previous 7 days, and amount > 5× their mean |
| `no_history_high_amount` | no card transactions in the previous 7 days, and a high amount |

A transaction is blocked if any rule fires.

**Thresholds are derived, not typed.** `configs/rules.yaml` fixes quantile *levels* (for example
0.99). The thresholds come from **legitimate transactions in the train window only**, and are then
frozen. On the synthetic data that produced `high_amount` > 304.98 USD and `velocity_1h` > 1
transaction. A unit test changes every amount after the train window and checks that the thresholds
do not move.

**Worked example: synthetic `test` window (43,738 transactions, 0.83% fraud).**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Block rate | 1.96% |
| Precision | 33.64% |
| Recall (count) | 79.56% |
| Fraud value caught | 93.89% (55,077 of 58,664 USD) |
| False-decline rate | 1.31% |
| PR-AUC (score = rules fired) | 0.3286, against a no-skill value of 0.0083 |
| Loss: missed fraud + false declines | 3,587 + 22,067 = **25,655 USD** |

Reading it:

- **False declines dominate the loss** (22,067 of 25,655 USD). The rules catch most fraud value by
  blocking many good customers. With the assumed costs (10% margin + 2 USD per declined good
  customer), turning away legitimate business costs more than the fraud that slips through. That
  is exactly the gap a model with a *review* action can close (ADR-005, Phase 4).
- **Evaluating each rule alone shows what each is worth:**

| Rule alone | Precision | Recall | Value caught |
|---|---|---|---|
| `velocity_1h` | 94.26% | 63.54% | 39.80% |
| `high_amount` | 10.39% | 14.64% | 84.37% |
| `amount_spike` | 46.60% | 13.26% | 60.05% |
| `new_device_high_amount` | 100.00% | 8.29% | 26.07% |
| `no_history_high_amount` | 1.18% | 0.28% | 0.73% |

`velocity_1h` catches card-testing bursts almost without false positives. `high_amount` is
imprecise but catches most fraud *value* (the cash-outs). `no_history_high_amount` is close to
useless on this data. The combined score hides that; the per-rule breakdown makes it visible.

## Why we chose it here

- **Baseline discipline (ADR-002).** LightGBM is justified only by beating simpler options, and rules
  are the simplest.
- **Money, not F1 (ADR-005).** The same harness (`bastion.evaluation`) scores rules now and models
  and the policy engine later, so the comparison is like for like.
- **Temporal honesty (ADR-007).** Thresholds are fit on the train window and reported on `test`,
  exactly like a model.

## What we rejected and why

- **Hand-typed thresholds** ("amount > 500"). They are magic numbers that nobody can reproduce or
  justify, and they get quietly re-tuned after looking at results.
- **Tuning thresholds on the test window.** That is leakage, and it makes the baseline look better
  than a live rules engine could be.
- **Skipping the baseline.** The first model's PR-AUC would then have no reference point.
- **Scoring rules with F1 or accuracy.** Neither accounts for how much the missed or declined
  transactions were worth.

## Where it breaks

- **Synthetic rules are flattered.** The rules and the generator were written by the same person;
  `velocity_1h` is excellent because the card-testing bursts were designed to be bursts. Expect
  much weaker IEEE-CIS results (unmeasured).
- **Quantiles of legitimate traffic drift.** If legitimate spending changes, thresholds fit on old
  data flag the wrong customers, and nothing re-fits them automatically.
- **Adversaries adapt.** Once fraudsters learn the velocity limit, they probe just below it. Rules
  have no way to generalise past the patterns they encode.
- **Block-only decisions.** Rules here never send a case to review, so every uncertain call becomes
  either a missed fraud or a false decline.

## The interview answer

"Before training anything, I built five rules and measured them in money on the same temporal test
window a model would face. Their thresholds came from quantiles of legitimate training traffic and
were frozen, so nothing was tuned on test. On my synthetic data they caught about 94% of fraud value,
but most of the loss came from blocking good customers. That told me the model's job isn't just
higher recall: it's cheaper decisions, and a review action. Every model since has had to beat that
loss figure."
