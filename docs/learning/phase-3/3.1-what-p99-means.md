# 3.1 What p99 means and why the mean lies

**Phase:** 3 · **Date:** 2026-09-15 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** Every latency here was measured by `bastion bench latency` on one laptop
> (12th Gen Intel i7-1255U, `powersave` governor, on AC power). k6, the service and Redis all shared
> that machine. The numbers come from 30-second runs on synthetic payloads, so they describe this
> setup, not a production system. Full reports:
> [before the optimisation](../../results/phase3/before/latency.md),
> [after](../../results/phase3/latency.md).

## What problem does this solve?

ARCHITECTURE §3.3 gives the scoring path a budget of 50 ms at p99. To say whether the service meets
it, I first had to be precise about which single number describes "latency". The obvious one, the
average, can look fine while the budget is broken.

## How it works

**A percentile is a position in the sorted list.** Sort every request's latency: p50 is the value in
the middle, p99 the value 99% of the way up. p99 = 33 ms means one request in a hundred took at
least 33 ms. At 400 requests per second, that is four payments every second.

**The mean folds the tail into the body and hides it.** Here is the first benchmark run at 400
requests/s, 12,001 requests, before the Phase 3 optimisation:

| Statistic | Client-side latency |
|---|---|
| mean | 4.61 ms |
| p50 | 3.36 ms |
| p95 | 9.17 ms |
| p99 | 33.16 ms |
| max | 100.16 ms |

The mean says "under 5 ms, a tenth of the budget". But p99 is seven times the mean, and the slowest
request took twice the whole budget. A few hundred slow requests barely move an average over 12,001,
which is exactly why the average cannot be used to control them.

**Where the tail came from.** The service runs one worker process with one asyncio event loop.
Feature evaluation is CPU work, so while one request evaluates features, every other request's Redis
reply waits to be processed. The server-side stage timings from the decision log are consistent
with that: at 400 requests/s the Redis stage had p50 0.67 ms but p99 21.28 ms. That stage includes
the wait for the event loop, and a p99 thirty times its median points to queueing rather than a slow
store (3.2 tests this). Queueing is non-linear: as utilisation nears 100%, waiting time grows much
faster than load. At 800 requests/s the service could not keep up at all. p50 was 343 ms, 36% of
requests failed, and k6 could not even start 267 of them.

**Measuring without fooling yourself.** Two choices in the benchmark matter as much as the statistic:

1. **Open load model.** k6's `constant-arrival-rate` executor schedules requests at a fixed rate,
   whether or not earlier ones have finished, as independent payment traffic would. A closed model
   (N virtual users, each waiting for its response) sends *less* load when the server slows down.
   The requests that would have queued are then never sent and never measured. That error is called
   *coordinated omission*.
2. **Call a rate sustained only if it really was.** Every scheduled request started, none failed, and
   the achieved rate stayed within 2% of the target. The report's headline is the highest sustained
   rate. Rates that failed still appear in the table, marked as not sustained.

The report pairs client-side latency (from k6) with server-side stage timings for the same requests
(from the decision log), so a tail can be traced to a stage.

## Why we chose it here

- ARCHITECTURE §3.3 states the budget as a p99, and ROADMAP Phase 3 asks for p50, p95, p99 and
  throughput.
- A fraud check sits inside the payment path. Customers experience individual requests, not
  averages: one slow check per hundred payments is a steady stream of slow checkouts at volume.
- Dean and Barroso's point in *The Tail at Scale*: when one user action fans out to many services,
  the tail of each service becomes the *typical* experience of the whole. The scoring call is one
  such service.

## What we rejected and why

- **Reporting the mean, or mean ± standard deviation.** Latency distributions are skewed with a long
  right tail; a standard deviation describes a symmetric spread that isn't there.
- **A closed-model load test** (fixed virtual users in a loop). Coordinated omission makes the tail
  look best exactly when it is worst.
- **Timing one request with `timeit`.** It measures a single request on an idle process. Without
  concurrent requests there is no queueing, and queueing is where the tail comes from.
- **Averaging percentiles across runs.** Percentiles do not average. Every row of the report is
  computed from that run's own samples.

## Where it breaks

- **One machine.** k6, the service and Redis share twelve logical CPUs, so the load generator
  competes with what it measures. A proper benchmark would put the client on separate hardware.
- **Short runs.** 30 seconds at 400 requests/s gives 12,000 samples. That is 120 above p99, and only
  12 above p99.9, too few to report.
- **CPU frequency scaling.** In the first run, latency *fell* as load rose from 25 to 200 requests/s
  (p50 7.30 → 2.96 ms), and the server-side stages slowed down the same way at low rates. The likely
  cause is the `powersave` governor leaving cores at low frequency when lightly loaded. That is a
  hypothesis, not a verified cause.
- **Synthetic payloads.** History sizes per card come from the synthetic generator. A card with
  thousands of recent events would make feature evaluation slower.
- **One client, keep-alive connections.** Real traffic arrives from many clients over many
  connections.

## The interview answer

"I report latency as percentiles from an open-model load test, because the mean hides the tail. In
my first run at 400 requests per second, the mean was 4.6 ms, but p99 was 33 ms and the slowest
request took 100 ms, twice the budget. The stage timings pointed at requests waiting on the single
event loop while other requests did CPU-bound feature work, not at Redis. I used k6's
constant-arrival-rate executor, so a slow server doesn't get less load, which avoids coordinated
omission. A rate only counts as sustained if nothing was dropped or failed."
