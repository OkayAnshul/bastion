# 4.3 Why three actions beat two

**Phase:** 4 · **Date:** 2026-09-15 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** Every number here is arithmetic on the *assumed* costs in
> `configs/costs.yaml`: a false decline costs 10% of the amount plus 2 USD, a review costs 5 USD, and
> analysts stop 90% of the fraud they review. `test_the_review_band_widens_with_the_amount` checks
> the bands. Nothing here is a measurement of fraud.

## What problem does this solve?

A binary fraud system has two ways to be wrong: approve a fraud or decline a customer. For a
transaction the model is unsure about, both are expensive, and all the system can do is pick which
mistake it prefers. ADR-005 adds a third action, human review. Its fixed cost buys most of the
certainty the model lacks.

## How it works

With expected costs `approve = p·a`, `review = r + p·(1 - c)·a` and `block = (1 - p)·(m·a + f)`,
each pair of actions has a crossover probability:

| Comparison | The second action is cheaper when |
|---|---|
| review vs approve | `p > r / (c·a)` |
| review vs block | `p < (m·a + f - r) / (m·a + f + (1 - c)·a)` |
| block vs approve | `p > (m·a + f) / (a + m·a + f)` |

Review is the best action between the first two crossovers, if that interval exists. With the
assumed costs:

| Amount | Approve | Review | Block |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 USD | p < 0.167 | never | p > 0.167 |
| 200 USD | p < 0.028 | 0.028 < p < 0.405 | p > 0.405 |
| 5,000 USD | p < 0.0011 | 0.0011 < p < 0.496 | p > 0.496 |

What the table shows:

- **Small payments are never worth a review.** For 20 USD, the most a review can save is less than
  the 5 USD it costs, so the decision stays binary, at p ≈ 0.17.
- **The review band widens with the amount.** At 5,000 USD, a 0.2% chance of fraud already justifies
  a 5 USD look. The band's upper edge rises towards 0.5, where blocking takes over.
- **Two actions force a bad trade in the middle.** A binary 200 USD decision at p = 0.2 either
  approves (expected cost 40 USD) or blocks (17.60 USD). A review costs 9 USD.

A probability threshold cannot draw these amount-dependent bands. That is why the expected-loss
policy prices actions per transaction, and why a budget-constrained review threshold is expressed
in money saved, not in probability (4.1).

## Why we chose it here

- ADR-005 names review as "the highest-value action": human judgment spent only where the model is
  unsure and the amount is worth it.
- The evaluation can say exactly what review bought. The same policy with a budget of zero is the
  binary system, so the report's budget-0 row is the two-action baseline.

## What we rejected and why

- **Approve/block only.** It is the budget-0 row, kept for comparison. The middle of the probability
  range is where it loses money.
- **Review everything above a probability.** This ignores the amount. It sends 20 USD payments to
  analysts who cost more than they can save, and approves a doubtful 5,000 USD payment once the band
  is used up.
- **Step-up authentication (for example 3-D Secure) as a fourth action.** Real payment systems use
  it, and it may be cheaper than review for card-not-present fraud. But the data has no outcomes for
  challenged payments, so its costs would be pure invention.

## Where it breaks

- **Review is not free for the customer.** A held payment delays the purchase. That friction isn't
  costed, so the band is wider than it should be.
- **Analysts are not the assumed perfect-on-legit, 90%-on-fraud reviewers.** A lower catch rate
  shrinks the band. The policy report re-tunes at 70% and 100% to show the effect.
- **Capacity is finite.** The unconstrained band may hold more cases than analysts can review, so
  the budget and its threshold (4.1) decide which of them actually get a review.
- **Probabilities near the band edges** are where calibration errors flip the action.

## The interview answer

"Two actions force a choice between declining customers and approving fraud whenever the model is
unsure. Adding review changes the maths. Approving costs p times the amount, blocking costs the
chance the customer is legitimate times margin and friction, and review costs a fixed analyst fee
plus the fraud analysts miss. That gives a review band between two crossover probabilities, and the
band depends on the amount. With my assumed costs, a 20 USD payment is never worth reviewing, a
200 USD payment is reviewed between roughly 3% and 40%, and a 5,000 USD payment between 0.1% and
50%. A single probability threshold can't express that, so the policy prices each action per
transaction."
