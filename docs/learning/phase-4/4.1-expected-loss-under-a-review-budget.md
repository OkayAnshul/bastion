# 4.1 Expected loss under a review budget

**Phase:** 4 · **Date:** 2026-09-15 · **Time spent:** written during the build

> **About the numbers.** The worked examples are arithmetic on the *assumed* costs in
> `configs/costs.yaml`, and unit tests check them. The only measured run is on the synthetic
> benchmark table. It shows the machinery works end to end, not how much real fraud it would catch.
> IEEE-CIS results: unmeasured.

## What problem does this solve?

A model outputs a probability, but a payment system needs an action: approve, send to a human, or
block. The textbook move, a threshold at 0.5 or wherever F1 peaks, treats every error as equal. A
missed 5 USD fraud and a missed 5,000 USD fraud count the same, and so does declining a loyal
customer. ADR-005 asks for something else: lose as little money as possible, given that analysts can
only review so many cases a day.

## How it works

**1. Price every action.** With a calibrated fraud probability `p`, an amount `a`, and the assumed
costs (margin share `m`, friction `f`, review cost `r`, analyst catch rate `c`):

| Action | Expected cost | Why |
|---|---|---|
| approve | `p · a` | an approved fraud is charged back in full |
| review | `r + p · (1 - c) · a` | analysts cost `r` per case and miss a share `1 - c` of fraud |
| block | `(1 - p) · (m · a + f)` | a blocked legitimate customer costs margin plus friction |

These are the same charges the evaluation makes once the label is known, so each expected cost is
the real loss averaged over the probability. A unit test checks exactly that.

**2. Without a budget, take the cheapest action.** Per transaction, that minimises total expected
loss. Section 4.3 shows the "review" band this creates and why it widens with the amount.

**3. With a budget, the obvious rule wastes reviews.** The team can review N cases per day, and the
service must decide in real time, in the order payments arrive. Reviewing every case that wants a
review, until the day's N are gone, spends capacity on whatever comes first. The unit test's
example, with a budget of one review:

| | Five 200 USD payments at p = 0.3, then one 5,000 USD payment at p = 0.3 | Expected cost |
|---|---|---|
| Greedy | reviews the first 200 USD payment; the large one arrives after the budget is gone and is blocked | 424 USD |
| Threshold | blocks the small ones and keeps the review for the large one | 232 USD |

**4. One threshold fixes it.** Review only when a review is expected to save more than τ compared
with the better of approving and blocking. τ is tuned separately for each budget on the calibration
window, by minimising total expected cost with the budget applied in event order. Tuning needs no
labels, because expected cost comes from the probabilities. The price is that tuning trusts the
calibration completely.

**5. Evaluate once, with labels.** On the test window the report gives fraud value caught, the
false-decline rate, reviews used per day, and the realized loss, for every budget in
`configs/policy.yaml`. It sets these against:

- two probability thresholds, tuned on the same objective;
- the Phase 0 rules;
- approving everything.

That gives the ROADMAP's sentence: "at N reviews/day we catch X% of fraud value at a Y% false-decline
rate".

**What the synthetic run taught.** On the synthetic benchmark table (`bastion policy sweep`, 17 s),
allowing reviews *raised* the realized test loss: 453 USD, against 26 USD with a budget of zero. Yet
the model's expected loss said the opposite: 347 USD against 759 USD.

- **With reviews:** the policy used 10 reviews in 29 days (50 USD of analyst time). The assumed 10%
  of reviewed fraud that analysts miss cost 403 USD.
- **With no budget:** those transactions were approved or blocked instead. That caught all the fraud
  value, with 26 USD of false declines.

The mid-probability cases the policy chose to review turned out to be fraud. Expected-loss tuning
leans hardest on calibration in exactly the probability band with the fewest examples. On ten cases
that is noise. At scale it is a real risk to measure, not assume away.

## Why we chose it here

- ADR-005 makes monetary loss under review capacity the objective, and fraud value caught at a
  review budget the headline metric.
- Elkan's point in *The Foundations of Cost-Sensitive Learning*: with calibrated probabilities and a
  cost matrix, the optimal decision follows from the costs, with no retraining. A threshold on
  probability alone is only optimal when every example has the same costs, and amounts break that.
- The budget is applied in event order with one counter per UTC day. That is what the scoring
  service can actually do, so offline and online decisions follow one rule.

## What we rejected and why

- **A fixed probability cutoff (0.5, or F1-optimal).** It ignores amounts and costs. It survives only
  as the baseline, and even then tuned on expected loss.
- **The top N probabilities of each day.** Online, the day's other transactions haven't happened
  yet, so you cannot rank the day before deciding.
- **Tuning τ on realized loss with labels.** Honest in one way, but it needs matured labels for the
  tuning window, and with a handful of frauds it is noisy. Expected loss needs no labels but trusts
  calibration. The report shows both numbers side by side, *total loss* and *model-expected loss*, so
  a disagreement is visible.
- **Classifier training with class weights for cost.** Reweighting distorts probabilities, and the
  costs change more often than the model. Here costs live in configuration and are applied after
  calibration.

## Where it breaks

- **The costs are assumptions.** IEEE-CIS carries no merchant economics. The report's sensitivity
  table re-tunes under halved and doubled costs and different catch rates, which shows the direction
  of each assumption but proves none of them.
- **Calibration where it matters.** Mid-probability transactions are rare, which is where the
  calibrator has the least data and review decisions are made.
- **Event-order spending.** A real team can defer a case to tomorrow, or set shifts that don't start
  at midnight UTC. Neither is modelled.
- **Analysts are idealised.** They clear every legitimate case and stop a fixed share of fraud.
- **Adversaries adapt.** A threshold tuned on last month's fraud is fitted to last month's attackers.

## The interview answer

"Instead of thresholding the fraud probability, I price each action per transaction: approving costs
p times the amount, blocking costs the chance the customer is legitimate times the lost margin and
friction, and reviewing costs the analyst's time plus the fraud they miss. The cheapest action wins.
Because analysts can only review N cases a day and decisions happen in real time, a greedy rule
spends the budget on whatever arrives first. So a review also has to save more than a threshold,
which I tune per budget on a calibration window by expected cost, without labels. The evaluation then
reports fraud value caught and false-decline rate at each budget, on a later window, against a
probability-threshold baseline, the rules, and approving everything."
